Silly rules: why pointless taboos can help a group

Eight learning agents forage for berries. Pink berries are poisonous, but the harm shows up long after eating, so no agent can tell which berry caused it. A taboo means that anyone who breaks it gets marked, the mark is visible only to others, and others are rewarded for punishing marked agents. The question: does adding a second, harmless taboo (teal) help or hurt?

A simplified, inspectable model of Köster, Hadfield-Menell, Everett, Weidinger, Hadfield & Leibo (2022), “Spurious normativity enhances learning of compliance and enforcement behavior in artificial agents,” PNAS 119(3). Not deep RL: each agent learns a handful of numbers you can inspect.

Changing a setting starts a new run. All three worlds use the same random seed, so they differ only in their rules.

Things to try

  1. Watch the order. In the rule worlds, the number of violators punished rises before time spent poisoned falls. The group learns to enforce first, and only then do individuals learn to comply.
  2. Run the experiment. A single run is noisy. Averaged over populations, look at episodes 3–12: silly-rule worlds build enforcement skill faster and get out of the poison sooner, because twice as many violations means twice the practice. Then look at group return: the silly rule's early punishment spike costs points, and in this model the payback is real but modest. That matches the paper, where the welfare advantage was confined to a middle stretch of training.
  3. Test the mechanism. Set enforcement skill to separate for each norm and rerun the experiment. Practice on teal no longer helps with pink, and the silly rule loses its advantage.
  4. Price the punishment. Faster compliance comes at a cost: more punishments, each costing the group points. Make punishment cheap (a raised eyebrow) or expensive (a duel) and watch whether the silly rule pays for itself.
  5. Remove the credit-assignment problem. Set the poison delay near 0. Now agents can work out by themselves which berry made them sick, and the no-rules world learns on its own. Norms matter when consequences are delayed and causes are unclear.
